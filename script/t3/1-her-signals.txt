The hook is three questions, from Emma's slides. One: does it skip a generation? Two: is there a difference between the sexes? Three: who are the homozygous recessives? Then a Punnett square.

Step one, read the chart. Squares are males. Circles are females. Shaded means affected. Half shaded, or a dot, means a carrier. A line through a symbol means the person has died. The generations are Roman numerals down the side, and the people are numbered from left to right. And when you check the generations, leave out the people who married in.

Step two, dominant or recessive? If the trait appears in every generation and never skips, it's dominant. If it skips a generation, it's recessive. The giveaway for recessive is an affected child with two unaffected parents.

Step three, autosomal or X-linked? Count the affected males and the affected females. About equal: autosomal. A clear difference: sex-linked. And one quick test: if a father passes it to his son, it can't be X-linked, because a son gets his father's Y, not his X.

[pause 1]

The X-linked rules, in case the chart is lopsided. In X-linked recessive, fathers can't pass it to their sons, and an affected mother passes it to all of her sons. In X-linked dominant, an affected father passes it to all of his daughters.

Step four, genotypes. Label the homozygous recessives first: little b, little b. Everyone else has at least one dominant allele, and you work out their second one from their parents and their children. In her example, the affected father must be big B little b, because some of his children are unaffected, little b little b, and each of them got a little b from him. The unaffected mother is little b little b.

Step five, the chance. Draw a Punnett square of the parents. Big B little b, crossed with little b little b. Two boxes are big B little b, and two boxes are little b little b. So each child has a fifty per cent chance of being affected.

Another one from her help board, and she sums them up in a few words each. Glaucoma: the pressure in the eye. Cataract: the clarity of the lens.

The hook: glaucoma is a pressure problem, because the drain blocks. A cataract is a clarity problem, because the lens goes cloudy.

Glaucoma first. It involves the aqueous humour, the watery fluid at the front of the eye. Normally the ciliary body makes it and the canal of Schlemm drains it, at the same rate. If the canal of Schlemm is blocked, or too much fluid is made, aqueous humour builds up in the anterior segment, and the intraocular pressure rises. That pressure compresses the retina and its blood supply, and damages the optic nerve. So vision is lost, and it can end in blindness.

[pause 1]

Now the cataract. A cataract affects the lens. The proteins in the lens denature, so the lens turns cloudy, or opaque, instead of clear. The cloudy lens stops light reaching the retina properly, so vision becomes blurred and yellowed, with poor night vision and sensitivity to glare. It's common with age and with diabetes. And it's treated with surgery that replaces the lens.

[pause 1]

The traps. Don't swap them. Glaucoma is pressure: the aqueous humour and the canal of Schlemm. A cataract is the lens. Second: the ciliary body makes the fluid, and the canal of Schlemm drains it. Third: in glaucoma the lens is fine. It's the retina and the optic nerve that get damaged.
