# The three regions of the ear
@ crit: SS5
@ saq: ear-regions
@ say: The three regions of the ear

Q: The three regions of the ear. Four and a half marks.

Q: What are the three regions of the ear? Describe their structures and their functions.

Four and a half marks means about nine half-mark facts. Three regions, three facts each.

The hook: the outer ear collects, the middle ear amplifies, and the inner ear senses. And the inner ear does two jobs: the cochlea hears, and the vestibular apparatus balances.

The outer ear, or external ear. The pinna, the external auditory canal, and the tympanic membrane, which is the eardrum. Glands in the canal make cerumen, which is earwax. Its function: it collects sound and directs it onto the eardrum, and the pinna also helps you locate where a sound is coming from.

The middle ear. An air-filled cavity in the temporal bone, holding the ossicles: the malleus, the incus and the stapes. Its function: it amplifies sound, passing it from the large eardrum to the much smaller oval window. It also has the Eustachian tube, also called the pharyngotympanic tube, running to the nasopharynx, which equalises pressure. And two small muscles, the tensor tympani and the stapedius, which dampen loud sounds.

[pause 1]

The inner ear. The bony labyrinth in the temporal bone, filled with perilymph, and inside it the membranous labyrinth, filled with endolymph. It has two parts. The cochlea, for hearing: its organ of Corti turns sound vibration into nerve impulses. And the vestibular apparatus, which is the vestibule and the semicircular canals, for equilibrium, or balance.

[pause 1]

The traps. Only the inner ear does balance. The outer and middle ear are for hearing only. Second: the Eustachian tube equalises pressure. It's why your ears pop on a plane. It doesn't carry sound. Third: perilymph is in the bony labyrinth, on the outside. Endolymph is inside the membranous labyrinth. Endo means inside.

Q: Once more. The three regions of the ear.

[pause 2]

{recap} The outer ear: the pinna, the external auditory canal with its cerumen, and the tympanic membrane. It collects sound onto the eardrum and helps locate it. The middle ear: air-filled, in the temporal bone, with the ossicles, which amplify sound onto the oval window. The Eustachian tube equalises pressure, and two small muscles dampen loud sounds. The inner ear: a bony labyrinth of perilymph around a membranous labyrinth of endolymph. The cochlea, with its organ of Corti, hears. The vestibular apparatus balances.

# How hearing happens
@ crit: SS6
@ saq: hearing
@ say: How hearing happens

Q: How hearing occurs. Four marks.

Q: Discuss how hearing occurs in humans, from the sound wave arriving at the ear, to the sound being perceived.

This one is a chain. Get the links in order, and the marks fall out.

The hook: collect, amplify, ripple, bend, fire, hear.

Collect. In the outer ear, the pinna collects sound waves and channels them along the external auditory canal to the tympanic membrane, which vibrates.

Amplify. In the middle ear, the vibration passes to the ossicles: malleus, then incus, then stapes. They amplify it onto the much smaller oval window.

Ripple. In the inner ear, the oval window vibrates the perilymph in the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani, and that passes the vibration on to the endolymph in the scala media, the cochlear duct.

[pause 1]

Bend. The basilar membrane moves, and the hair cells of the organ of Corti bend against the tectorial membrane.

Fire. Bending the hairs is transduction. The hair cells are mechanoreceptors, and they turn the vibration into nerve impulses.

Hear. The impulses travel along the cochlear branch of the vestibulocochlear nerve, cranial nerve eight, to the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe, where the sound is heard.

[pause 1]

The traps. It's the middle ear that amplifies: the ossicles. Her student-marked feedback slips and says the inner ear. Her deck says the middle ear, so write middle. Second: the hair cells sit on the basilar membrane, and bend against the tectorial membrane. Third: the auditory cortex is in the temporal lobe. The occipital lobe is vision.

Q: Once more. How hearing happens.

[pause 2]

{recap} The pinna collects sound down the canal, and the tympanic membrane vibrates. The ossicles, malleus, incus and stapes, amplify it onto the oval window. That moves the perilymph, then the endolymph, and the basilar membrane moves, so the hair cells of the organ of Corti bend against the tectorial membrane. That's transduction into nerve impulses, which travel along nerve eight to the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe.

# Static and dynamic equilibrium
@ crit: SS8, SS9
@ saq: equilibrium
@ say: Static and dynamic equilibrium

Q: Static versus dynamic equilibrium. Four marks.

Q: What is the difference between static and dynamic equilibrium? How, and where, is each one detected?

The hook: static is gravity and straight lines, in the vestibule, where little stones slide. Dynamic is spinning, in the semicircular canals, where fluid swings a gel cap.

What they share. Both are sensed in the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear, by hair cells, which are mechanoreceptors. The hair cells fire when fluid, the endolymph, or a jelly mass moves and bends their hairs.

Static equilibrium is the position of your head in response to gravity, and to straight-line movement. Tilting your head. Moving forwards or backwards. Going up in a lift. It's detected by the maculae, in the utricle and the saccule, which together make up the vestibule. The hairs there are embedded in a jelly otolith membrane, studded with tiny stones called otoliths. When you tilt your head, the stones slide and bend the hairs.

[pause 1]

Dynamic equilibrium is balance while your head is rotating: turning, or spinning. It's detected by the crista ampullaris, in the ampulla at the base of each semicircular canal. Rotation moves the endolymph, which swings a gel cap called the cupula, and that bends the hairs.

Both send impulses along the vestibular branch of cranial nerve eight, to the brain stem and the cerebellum. And signals that fire with no real movement cause vertigo.

[pause 1]

The traps. Static is the vestibule: the utricle, the saccule, the maculae, the otoliths. Dynamic is the semicircular canals: the ampulla, the crista, the cupula. Swap them and you lose both marks. Second: static doesn't mean no movement at all. A lift going up is static, because it's a straight line. Dynamic means rotation. Third: the cochlea is for hearing, not balance.

Q: Once more. Static and dynamic equilibrium.

[pause 2]

{recap} Both are sensed by hair cells in the vestibular apparatus of the inner ear, and sent along nerve eight to the brain stem and cerebellum. Static equilibrium is head position relative to gravity, and straight-line movement. It's detected by the maculae in the utricle and saccule, where otoliths slide when the head tilts. Dynamic equilibrium is balance during rotation. It's detected by the crista ampullaris in each semicircular canal, where the endolymph swings the cupula and bends the hairs.

Q: The three layers of the eye. Four and a half marks.

Q: What are the three layers, or tunics, of the eye? Give their structures and their functions.

The hook, from the outside in: the tough coat, the blood coat, and the seeing coat.

The tough coat is the fibrous tunic, on the outside. It has two parts. The white sclera, which protects the eye and anchors the muscles that move it. And the clear cornea at the front, which lets light in, and does about eighty per cent of the refraction.

The blood coat is the vascular tunic, also called the uvea, in the middle. It has three parts. The choroid: pigmented and rich in blood vessels. It nourishes the other layers, and stops light scattering. The ciliary body: its ciliary muscles and suspensory ligaments hold the lens and change its shape, and its processes make the aqueous humour. And the iris: its circular and radial muscles control the size of the pupil, and so how much light gets in.

[pause 1]

The seeing coat is the neural tunic, the retina, on the inside. Its outer pigmented layer absorbs light and stops it scattering. Its inner neural layer has three layers of neurons: the photoreceptors, rods and cones, then bipolar cells, then ganglion cells, whose axons leave the eye as the optic nerve, cranial nerve two. The photoreceptors turn light into nerve impulses.

[pause 1]

The traps. The cornea, not the lens, does most of the refraction: about eighty per cent. Second: the choroid belongs to the middle, vascular tunic, not the retina. Third: the rods and cones are in the neural layer. The pigmented layer only absorbs light.

Q: Once more. The three layers of the eye.

[pause 2]

{recap} The fibrous tunic: the sclera, which protects the eye and anchors its muscles, and the cornea, which does about eighty per cent of the refraction. The vascular tunic: the choroid, rich in blood vessels, which nourishes the eye; the ciliary body, which holds the lens, changes its shape, and makes aqueous humour; and the iris, which controls pupil size. The neural tunic, the retina: a pigmented layer that absorbs light, then photoreceptors, rods and cones, then bipolar cells, then ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve.

# Near vision: the three changes

Q: Near vision. Three marks.

Q: What changes occur in the eye to allow near vision? And how is this undone for distant vision?

Three marks, three changes. Picture yourself reading a book held close to your face.

The hook: bulge, shrink, cross. The lens bulges, the pupils shrink, and the eyes cross inwards. Look out of the window, and all three undo.

Bulge. The ciliary muscle contracts, the suspensory ligaments go slack, and the elastic lens recoils into a rounder shape, so it bends light more. That's accommodation of the lens.

Shrink. The pupils constrict. The circular, sphincter muscles of the iris contract.

Cross. The eyes converge: both eyeballs rotate medially, turning inwards toward the near object.

[pause 1]

Why it's needed. Light from a near object arrives as diverging rays. Light from more than six metres away arrives as parallel rays. The near rays need more bending to land on the retina.

For distant vision, all three undo. The ciliary muscle relaxes, the lens flattens, the pupils dilate, and the eyes look straight ahead.

[pause 1]

The traps. For near vision, the pupil constricts. Her student-marked feedback says it dilates, but her own quiz key and both of her decks say constricts. Write constricts. Second: the ciliary muscle contracts to make the lens bulge. Her true or false trap says it contracts to flatten the lens. That's false. Third: one of her slides calls the lens biconcave. It's biconvex.

Q: Once more. Near vision.

[pause 2]

{recap} The ciliary muscle contracts, so the lens bulges and gets rounder: accommodation. The pupils constrict. The eyes converge, turning inwards. For distant vision, all three undo: the ciliary muscle relaxes, the lens flattens, and the pupils dilate. Near light arrives diverging; from over six metres away it's parallel.

Q: Myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism. Five marks.

Q: Explain how myopia, emmetropia and hyperopia differ, and how each error is corrected. What is astigmatism?

The hook: myopia, my world is up close. Short-sighted. The focus falls short, in front of the retina, and a concave lens pushes it back. Hyperopia: the focus overshoots, behind the retina, and a convex lens pulls it forward.

Emmetropia is normal vision. Parallel light rays are focused on the retina.

Myopia is short-sightedness. Light from distant objects focuses in front of the retina, usually because the eyeball is too long. Near things are clear, and far things are blurred. It's corrected with a concave lens, which diverges the light, spreading it out, and moves the focus back onto the retina.

[pause 1]

Hyperopia is long-sightedness. Light from near objects focuses behind the retina, usually because the eyeball is too short. Far things are clearer than near ones. It's corrected with a convex lens, which converges the light and moves the focus forward onto the retina.

Astigmatism: the cornea or the lens is curved unevenly, more in one direction than another. So light focuses at several points, and vision is blurred at all distances. It's corrected with glasses or contact lenses, or with laser surgery.

[pause 1]

The traps. Myopia: short-sighted, concave. Hyperopia: long-sighted, convex. Swapping the lenses is the commonest slip. Second: say where the image falls, in front of or behind the retina. That's the mark, not just "blurry". Third: astigmatism doesn't depend on distance. It's blurred near and far.

Q: Once more. Myopia, hyperopia and astigmatism.

[pause 2]

# Waves: light and sound
@ crit: SS10, SS11
@ saq: waves
@ say: Waves of light and sound

Q: Waves of light and sound. Four marks.

Q: Explain amplitude, wavelength and frequency, and how we experience each one. Compare light and sound waves, and explain the difference between reflection and refraction of light.

The hook: amplitude is how tall, so loud or bright. Wavelength is peak to peak, so colour. Frequency is how many per second, so pitch. Reflection bounces, refraction bends.

Amplitude is the height of the wave: how far it moves from rest. In sound we experience it as loudness, measured in decibels. In light, as brightness.

Wavelength is the distance from one peak to the next. In light, it decides the colour we see. Visible light is measured in nanometres.

Frequency is the number of waves passing per second, measured in hertz. As frequency goes up, wavelength goes down. In sound, we hear frequency as pitch. Humans hear about twenty to twenty thousand hertz.

[pause 1]

Light versus sound. Light is electromagnetic, and needs no medium: it can cross a vacuum. Sound is a mechanical pressure wave, made of compressions and rarefactions, and it needs a medium: a gas, a liquid, or a solid.

Reflection: light bounces back off a flat, opaque surface, like a mirror. A coloured object reflects only its own wavelengths, and absorbs the rest.

Refraction: light bends when it passes into a medium of a different density, because it slows down there. A rainbow in raindrops. And in your own eye, the cornea and the lens refract light to focus it on the retina.

Her checklist says reflection and refraction of sound, too, and her keys cover it. Sound reflects: an echo is reflected sound, and so is sonar. And sound refracts: it bends upward, away from hot ground, because it travels faster in hot air than in cold air. While we're on rainbows, her model answer: raindrops act as tiny prisms, and each wavelength of light slows by a different amount, so blue, the shortest, bends the most, and red the least.

[pause 1]

The traps. Loudness is amplitude, and pitch is frequency. Her quizzes swap them to catch you. Second: a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength. Third: sound can't travel through a vacuum. Light can.

Q: Once more. Waves.

[pause 2]

{recap} Amplitude is the wave's height: loudness in decibels, or brightness. Wavelength is peak to peak: the colour of light. Frequency is waves per second, in hertz: the pitch of sound, and a higher frequency means a shorter wavelength. Light is electromagnetic and needs no medium; sound is a mechanical pressure wave that needs one. Reflection: light bounces back, like a mirror. Refraction: light bends as it slows in a different density, like a rainbow, or the cornea and lens focusing light. Sound reflects as an echo or sonar, and refracts, bending away from hot ground.

Q: The ear and sound, the fine print. Her worksheets, her tables, and her orders.

The written tracks walk you through the ear and through waves. Her worksheets and matching tables ask for more of the small parts, and for things in a particular order. This track is those.

The hook: air, bone, fluid. The oval window pushes, the round window gives. Low potassium outside, high potassium inside, and glutamate fires the nerve.

The outer ear. The pinna is made of cartilage, with the ear lobe at the bottom. It picks up sound waves and channels them down the ear canal, which her worksheet calls the external auditory meatus. The ceruminous glands in the canal make cerumen, a yellow-brown wax. If it packs the canal, the person struggles to hear: conduction deafness.

The middle ear. The middle ear cavity sits in the temporal bone. The tympanic membrane, the eardrum, is a relatively large, tough membrane that turns vibrations in air into vibrations in bone. The ossicles act as levers, to amplify the vibrations, and the stapes is the smallest bone in the human body. The Eustachian tube connects the middle ear to the throat, and equalises the pressure on the eardrum.

Her true or false: in the outer ear, sound travels through air; in the middle ear, through bone; and in the inner ear, through fluid. True. The oval window is where vibrations in bone become vibrations in fluid. And the round window: the oval window and the round window vibrate in opposite directions. As the stapes pushes the oval window in, the round window bulges out, so the fluid has room to move. Her order of the membranes: the tympanic membrane, the oval window, the basilar membrane, then the round window.

The inner ear. The cochlea is a fluid-filled, coiled tube, lined with the sensory cells of the organ of Corti, which turn vibrations in fluid into electrical impulses. The semicircular canals are fluid-filled, and set at right angles to each other, so they pick up movement in every direction. Two nerves leave the inner ear: the vestibular nerve, for balance, and the cochlear, or auditory, nerve, for hearing, which carries the impulses as action potentials to the temporal lobes.

Her organ of Corti sheet. Perilymph fills the scala vestibuli and the scala tympani, and it's low in potassium. Endolymph fills the scala media, and it's high in potassium. When the hairs bend, potassium and calcium flow into the top of the hair cell, and it releases the neurotransmitter glutamate, which sends an impulse on to the auditory cortex. Her other wording: the stereocilia bend against the tectorial membrane, excitatory neurotransmitter is released at the synapse with the sensory neurons, and action potentials travel to the central nervous system. Mechanical in, electrical out.

Her orders. Her worksheet's seven steps: movement of the oval window, movement of the perilymph in the scala tympani, movement of the basilar membrane, movement of the endolymph in the scala media, bending of the sensory hairs as the gel-like tectorial membrane moves, impulses along the cochlear nerve, and the auditory cortex in the temporal lobe. Notice that her orders put the basilar membrane before the endolymph. In her arrange-the-steps questions, use her order.

Pitch along the cochlea. Low sounds move the longer, floppier fibres, at the apex of the cochlear duct, furthest from the oval window. High-pitched sounds move the shorter, stiffer hair cells, at the base. Her receptors table says the same: high pitch, hair cells at the base of the cochlear duct; low pitch, hair cells at the apex.

Her light-and-sound table, ten marks, in two quizzes. Sound waves are mechanical waves: vibrations of particles, so they can't travel through a vacuum. Light waves are electromagnetic waves, so they can. Photons are light particles, or packages of light. The amplitude of light gives its intensity, how bright it is, measured in lux. The amplitude of sound gives its loudness, measured in decibels. The frequency and wavelength of light give its colour: red light has the lowest frequency, and violet the highest. Of sound, its pitch: a higher frequency is a higher pitch. And all light waves travel at the same speed, about three hundred thousand kilometres per second.

Her wave table's definitions. Wavelength: the distance between the crests of the waves, measured in nanometres for light. Frequency: how often a wave passes a point each second. Amplitude: the maximum displacement, or height, of the wave from its resting point.

Two more refraction wordings. A wave bends because one part of it slows down, or speeds up, relative to another part. Refraction goes into the new medium, but changes direction; reflection returns to the same medium. Her example of refraction: an object in water looks as if it's in a different position, like a straw that looks bent. Her example of reflection: a mirror image.

And three of her sense words. Static and dynamic equilibrium both work by mechanoreception. Taste and smell are both chemoreception, and her key says taste is eighty per cent smell.

The traps. The round window moves the opposite way to the oval window. Endolymph is high in potassium, perilymph is low. Low pitch is at the apex, high pitch at the base. And loud sound is amplitude, measured in decibels; bright light is amplitude too, measured in lux.
