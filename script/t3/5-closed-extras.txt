Q: The closed questions. The facts her quizzes ask that no other track covers.

About seventy per cent of Test 3's marks are closed questions: multiple choice, true or false, drop-downs and matching tables. Every other track in this episode is built around a written answer. This one picks up what's left: the one-line facts her quiz keys ask, most of them in two different quizzes, that no written answer covers. A few of them sit outside her checklist, like fertilisation, but her quizzes ask them anyway. No essays here. Just the fact, and the wording her key uses.

The hook: dark room, big pupils. Gentamicin breaks hair cells. And the corpus luteum makes three hormones for three months.

Genetics first, two facts. The chromosome conditions, the way her matching table pairs them. Down syndrome is trisomy twenty-one. Edwards syndrome is trisomy eighteen. Turner syndrome is a woman with only one X chromosome, forty-five in all, with underdeveloped female characteristics and a webbed neck. Klinefelter syndrome is a man with one Y and two or more X chromosomes, X X Y, forty-seven in all, with underdeveloped male characteristics. On a karyotype picture, Klinefelter is the one with a Y and two Xs. She shows you that picture in two quizzes.

And fetal testing. Her key, in two quizzes: the most common form of fetal testing is amniocentesis.

[pause 1]

The eye. First, the pupil's nerves. When you walk into a dark room, the pupils dilate, because the radial muscles of the iris contract, under sympathetic stimulation. In bright light, and for near vision, the pupils constrict: the circular muscles contract, under parasympathetic stimulation. So sympathetic means dilate, and her key adds that sympathetic is more predominant during distant vision. Think of being scared in the dark: sympathetic, big pupils.

The image, the way her drop-down runs. The lens is biconvex. So the image on the retina is real, and inverted: upside down. Through experience, the brain learns to turn it upright. The optic nerves form the optic chiasma, where half of the fibres from each eye cross to the opposite side of the brain. And the images from the two eyes are combined when the two halves of the brain talk through the corpus callosum.

Three one-mark words. The eyelids are the palpebrae. Lacrimal secretions are tears. And pinkeye is an infection of the conjunctiva: conjunctivitis.

[pause 1]

Her eye-conditions matching table is twelve marks, and it names five conditions no other track does. Blepharitis: inflammation of the tiny oil glands of the inner eyelids. Diabetic retinopathy: damage to the blood vessels of the retina, with floaters, blurriness, dark areas, and trouble seeing colours. Retinitis pigmentosa: hereditary, and the rods are destroyed first, so night blindness is the early sign. Dry macular degeneration: drusen build up between the choroid and the retina, and central vision blurs or is lost. Wet macular degeneration: the same, plus blood leaking out of the vessels. And her true or false: diabetes can cause cataracts, glaucoma and retinopathy. True.

Then her treatments table. Glaucoma: beta blockers, which slow down the making of aqueous humour. Conjunctivitis: the antibiotic chloramphenicol. Astigmatism: toric lenses, or rigid contact lenses. Retinal detachment: laser surgery, making small burns around the area. Cataract: surgery to remove the clouded lens and replace it. And dry macular degeneration: there's no effective treatment yet.

[pause 1]

The ear. Ototoxic drugs. Her help board files hearing loss from certain medications under sensorineural. Ototoxic means poisonous to the ear: these drugs damage the hair cells of the organ of Corti. Her list: aminoglycoside antibiotics like gentamicin, some TB drugs, the chemotherapy drug cisplatin, loop diuretics like furosemide, high-dose aspirin, and quinine. So her true or false, that gentamicin causes conduction deafness, is false: it's sensorineural. And when she lists causes of conductive hearing loss, ototoxic drugs are the one that doesn't belong.

Pitch along the cochlea. The basilar membrane changes in stiffness along its length, so each part answers a different frequency. High frequencies are picked up at the base, near the oval window. Low frequencies are picked up furthest from the oval window.

Sound shirts. Deaf people can feel music, because loud sound waves are vibrations of a medium, including our own tissues, and they're picked up by mechanoreceptors.

The Doppler effect: a change in the frequency, or the wavelength, of a wave, for an observer moving relative to its source. Her example: a siren sounds higher as the police car comes towards you, and lower as it drives away. Sitting in the car, you'd hear no change.

[pause 1]

Last, fertilisation and early pregnancy. They aren't on her checklist, but her quizzes ask them, so they're worth having.

Her fertilisation drop-down. On the way to the egg, the number of sperm falls a long way, but a male immune attack on the egg is not one of the reasons. That's the option to pick when she asks which one is NOT a reason. The egg is fertilised in the fallopian tube. The outer layer covering the egg, which comes from the follicle, her key calls the zona radiata. Pick that wording. Her figures call it the corona radiata, and the zona pellucida is the wrong option in that question. And the mineral the sperm needs to get through the egg's outer layers is calcium.

When conception is most likely: around ovulation, days ten to seventeen of the cycle. An ovulation predictor kit works by picking up the sharp peak of LH in the urine, just before ovulation. Her hormone tables add that oestrogen peaks just before ovulation too, and it's oestrogen that stimulates the release of LH. FSH makes the follicles grow.

[pause 1]

After fertilisation, the corpus luteum keeps going. For the first three months of pregnancy it makes three hormones: progesterone, oestrogen, and relaxin. After three months, the placenta takes over. If there's no pregnancy, it becomes the corpus albicans. So on her hormone graph, pregnancy removes the drop at about day twenty-one: oestrogen and progesterone keep rising. A pregnancy test looks for human chorionic gonadotropin, H C G. And the baby is an embryo up to eight weeks, and a foetus after that.

Contraception, her matching table. Condoms are a barrier that keeps semen out. An I U D may prevent implantation. Hormonal contraception may prevent ovulation. And the mini pill, the progesterone-only pill: it thickens the cervical mucus and thins the lining, but it does not release oxytocin or make the uterus contract. That's her wrong option.

Breast cancer, from her key. The risk factors are all of these: no breastfeeding, or only short periods of it; early periods and a late menopause; and a family history. And men can get breast cancer too, so they should check their breasts for lumps as well.

[pause 1]

The traps. Sympathetic dilates, parasympathetic constricts. Ototoxic drugs cause sensorineural deafness, never conduction. High pitch is at the base of the cochlea, by the oval window. Klinefelter is the man with an extra X. Turner is the woman with only one X. And the corpus luteum's three hormones are progesterone, oestrogen, and relaxin.
