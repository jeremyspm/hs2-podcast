Q: The male reproductive system. Ten marks.

Q: Label all the structures of the male reproductive system, and state their functions.

This is the male half of her biggest written question: ten marks on her student-marked quiz, and her model answer is a link to her own notes. So here are her notes, organ by organ, in the order the sperm travel. She'll give you a side-on diagram, so picture it as you listen.

The hook: make, store, carry, add, deliver. The testes make, the epididymis stores, the ducts carry, three glands add fluid, and the penis delivers.

The system's function: to produce the gametes, the sperm; to secrete the male sex hormones, mainly testosterone; and to deliver the sperm into the female reproductive tract.

The scrotum: a skin sac outside the body cavity, holding the two testes. Hanging outside keeps them about three degrees cooler than the core, which sperm production needs. Two muscles hold that temperature steady. The dartos is smooth muscle that wrinkles the skin, to cut heat loss. The cremaster is skeletal muscle that pulls the testes up towards the body when it's cold.

The testes: the male gonads. Inside, the seminiferous tubules produce the sperm. Between the tubules, the Leydig cells make testosterone. And the Sertoli cells nourish the developing sperm, and make inhibin.

[pause 1]

The epididymis: a cup-shaped duct on the back of each testis, with a head, a body and a tail. It stores the sperm, and it's where they mature and become motile. At ejaculation it contracts, and pushes them on into the vas deferens.

The vas deferens, also called the ductus deferens: the duct that carries the sperm from the epididymis up into the pelvis, where it widens into the ampulla. A vasectomy cuts the vas deferens, so sperm can't get from the testis to the outside, though fluid from the seminal vesicles and the prostate still leaves.

The seminal vesicles: paired glands on the back wall of the bladder. Each one joins its vas deferens to form an ejaculatory duct. They make about sixty per cent of the semen: a sticky, alkaline fluid, with fructose to feed the sperm, and prostaglandins.

The prostate: a doughnut-shaped gland under the bladder, wrapped around the urethra. It adds about a third of the semen: a milky fluid with citric acid as an energy source, enzymes, and prostate-specific antigen, PSA.

The bulbourethral glands, also called Cowper's glands: pea-sized, just below the prostate. Before ejaculation they release a thick, clear mucus that neutralises any traces of acidic urine in the urethra.

[pause 1]

The urethra: it carries both urine and semen, at different times, through the one opening. It has three parts: prostatic, through the prostate; membranous; and spongy, or penile, through the penis.

The penis: it delivers the sperm into the female reproductive tract. It has a root, a shaft, and the glans at the end, covered by the prepuce, the foreskin. Inside are three columns of erectile tissue: the two corpora cavernosa on top, and the corpus spongiosum, which surrounds the urethra and forms the glans.

So the path, the way she asks it: testis, epididymis, vas deferens, ampulla, ejaculatory duct, then the prostatic urethra, and out through the penis.

The traps. Sperm mature in the epididymis, not the testis. The dartos is smooth muscle and the cremaster is skeletal: her quiz offers both the wrong way round. And the cremaster lifts the testes in the cold, not in the heat.

[pause 2]

{recap} The scrotum keeps the testes three degrees cooler, with the dartos wrinkling the skin and the cremaster lifting the testes. The testes make sperm in the seminiferous tubules and testosterone in the Leydig cells, and the Sertoli cells nourish the sperm. The epididymis stores the sperm and matures them. The vas deferens carries them to the ampulla. The seminal vesicles add sixty per cent of the semen, alkaline, with fructose and prostaglandins, and form the ejaculatory ducts. Q: Semen, sperm count, erection and ejaculation.

Q: Describe the composition of semen, and the normal sperm count in relation to sterility. Then describe the physiology of the male sexual response, and relate it to erectile dysfunction.

Two checklist points. Her quizzes ask the semen part as match-the-gland, and the erection part as put-the-steps-in-order.

The hook for semen: sixty, thirty, ten. Sixty per cent from the seminal vesicles, thirty from the prostate, and ten from everything else: the sperm themselves, the testes, the epididymis, and the bulbourethral glands.

Semen is a milky, sticky mixture of sperm and the accessory gland fluids. It gives the sperm a transport medium; nutrients, fructose from the seminal vesicles and citrate from the prostate; protection; and it activates them. It's alkaline, which neutralises the acid in the male urethra and in the vagina. Its prostaglandins thin the mucus in the cervix, and set off reverse peristalsis in the uterus, which helps the sperm on their way. Clotting factors make it sticky straight after ejaculation, then an enzyme, fibrinolysin, liquefies it again.

The count. Only two to five millilitres is ejaculated, but it holds fifty to a hundred and thirty million sperm in every millilitre. Her quiz key: as many as seven hundred and fifty million sperm can be released in one ejaculation. Sterility is linked to a low count: below about twenty to twenty-five million per millilitre.

[pause 1]

Now the male sexual response. The hook: point and shoot. Parasympathetic for the erection, sympathetic for the ejaculation.

Erection. Sexual arousal sets off a parasympathetic reflex, which releases nitric oxide locally. Nitric oxide relaxes the smooth muscle in the vessel walls, so the arterioles dilate and the erectile tissue fills with blood. The corpora cavernosa expand, and that squashes the veins that drain them, so the blood stays in. The corpus spongiosum keeps the urethra open. An erection can be started, or stopped, by thoughts and emotions alone.

Ejaculation. Sympathetic nerves make the ducts and the accessory glands contract and empty into the urethra. The bladder sphincter closes, so no urine gets out. And the bulbospongiosus muscles contract rhythmically, pumping the semen out of the urethra.

Erectile dysfunction, or impotence, is the inability to attain an erection. Her slide adds how Viagra works: an enzyme called P D E 5 breaks down c G M P, the messenger that keeps the smooth muscle relaxed, and Viagra blocks P D E 5. So the vessels stay open.

Q: Once more. Semen, and the male sexual response.

[pause 2]

{recap} Semen is sixty per cent from the seminal vesicles, thirty from the prostate, and ten from the sperm and the rest. It carries, feeds, protects and activates the sperm, and it's alkaline. Two to five millilitres holds fifty to a hundred and thirty million sperm per millilitre, and a count below about twenty million is linked to sterility. Erection is parasympathetic: nitric oxide dilates the arterioles, the corpora cavernosa fill, and the veins are squashed shut. Ejaculation is sympathetic: the ducts contract, the bladder sphincter closes, and the bulbospongiosus pumps the semen out. Erectile dysfunction is being unable to attain an erection.

# Making sperm and eggs

Q: Making sperm and eggs. Four marks.

Q: Describe the gamete production process in males and in females. What hormone from the brain drives this?

The hook. Sperm start at puberty, never stop, and one cell makes four. Eggs start before birth, pause, go one a month, finish only if a sperm gets in, and one cell makes one.

Males first: spermatogenesis. It happens in the seminiferous tubules of the testes, continuously, from puberty on. Spermatogonia divide by mitosis into primary spermatocytes. Meiosis one gives secondary spermatocytes. Meiosis two gives spermatids. Then comes spermiogenesis: they lose most of their cytoplasm and grow a tail. One primary spermatocyte, four sperm.

[pause 1]

Females: oogenesis. It starts in fetal life. Oogonia divide by mitosis into primary oocytes, which begin meiosis one, and then stop, arrested in prophase one, until puberty. There are about two million of them at birth. From puberty, each month one primary oocyte finishes meiosis one, giving a secondary oocyte and the first polar body. The secondary oocyte arrests again, in metaphase two, and that's what is ovulated. Meiosis two is only completed if a sperm enters it, giving one ovum and a second polar body. And egg production ends at menopause.

The hormone: FSH, follicle-stimulating hormone, from the anterior pituitary, working together with LH.

[pause 1]

The traps. One primary spermatocyte makes four sperm. One primary oocyte makes only one egg; the rest are polar bodies. Second: eggs start before birth, and sperm start at puberty. Third: the egg only finishes meiosis two after a sperm gets in.

Q: Once more. Making sperm and eggs.

[pause 2]

{recap} Spermatogenesis happens in the seminiferous tubules, continuously from puberty. Spermatogonia divide by mitosis into primary spermatocytes, then meiosis one and two give spermatids, which become four sperm. Oogenesis starts in fetal life: primary oocytes arrest in prophase one until puberty. From puberty, one a month completes meiosis one and is ovulated, and meiosis two finishes only if a sperm enters, giving one ovum. Both are driven by FSH from the anterior pituitary, with LH.

Q: The ovarian and uterine cycles. Six marks.

Q: Describe the phases of the ovarian and uterine cycles in the female, and how the hormones link them.

She posted no model answer for this one, so this answer is built straight from her Repro Female deck. It's six marks, and it comes back in the final, in the fertility case, so it's worth the effort.

Two cycles run side by side: the ovary's, and the uterus's. Day one is the first day of the period.

The hook. The ovary: grow, pop, yellow body, white scar. The uterus: shed, rebuild, get ready.

The ovary first. The follicular phase, days one to fourteen. FSH from the anterior pituitary makes a follicle grow: primordial, primary, secondary, and then a mature Graafian follicle. Its granulosa cells secrete oestrogen.

Ovulation, mid-cycle, at about day fourteen. A surge of LH makes the Graafian follicle rupture and release the secondary oocyte. That's the pop.

The luteal phase, days fourteen to twenty-eight. The ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, the yellow body, which secretes progesterone, and some oestrogen. If there's no pregnancy, it breaks down after about ten days into the corpus albicans, the white scar.

[pause 1]

Now the uterus. The menstrual phase, days one to five. Progesterone falls, the spiral arteries spasm, and the stratum functionalis of the endometrium is shed.

The proliferative phase, days six to fourteen. Rising oestrogen from the growing follicle rebuilds the endometrium.

The secretory phase, days fifteen to twenty-eight. Progesterone from the corpus luteum turns the endometrium into a gland-rich lining, ready for implantation. If no embryo implants, the corpus luteum breaks down, progesterone falls, and menstruation starts again. That fall in progesterone is the link that restarts both cycles.

[pause 1]

The traps. The follicular phase goes with FSH and oestrogen. The luteal phase goes with the corpus luteum and progesterone. Second: proliferative is oestrogen, and secretory is progesterone. Third: only the stratum functionalis is shed. The stratum basalis stays, and grows the next one.

Q: Once more. Both cycles.

[pause 2]

{recap} The ovarian cycle. In the follicular phase, FSH makes a follicle grow into a Graafian follicle, which secretes oestrogen. At ovulation, around day fourteen, an LH surge ruptures it and releases the secondary oocyte. In the luteal phase the corpus luteum secretes progesterone, and without a pregnancy it becomes the corpus albicans. The uterine cycle. In the menstrual phase, progesterone falls and the stratum functionalis is shed. In the proliferative phase, oestrogen rebuilds it. In the secretory phase, progesterone readies it for implantation. With no implantation, progesterone falls, and menstruation starts again.

Her six differences. Duration: spermatogenesis is continuous from puberty to death, and oogenesis is monthly, from menarche to menopause.
