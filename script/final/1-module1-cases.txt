Q: Aleki and Vicky visit their friend Mary, who had a right mastectomy for cancer a fortnight ago. Mary mentions the cancer had spread to the lymph nodes, and some axillary lymph nodes were also removed. She complains of swelling in her right arm since the surgery. Her friend, who is good with mirimiri and massage, offers to massage her arm, which helps bring the swelling down.

Three questions, seven marks. It's the shortest case in the exam, so these are the easiest marks to bank.

Q: Three marks. What are the major functions of the lymphatic system?

Three functions, a mark each. The hook: digest, defend, drain.

Digestive. Lacteals in the gut absorb dietary fat, and carry it away for processing.

Immunological. The immune response. The thymus, where T cells mature. The bone marrow, where B cells mature, and B cells make antibodies. And the lymph nodes, where lymphocytes and other immune cells meet and coordinate an immune response.

Fluid balance. It drains away the excess tissue fluid, the interstitial fluid, monitors it for antigens on the way, and returns it to the venous system.

[pause 1]

Q: Two marks. What is the role of the axillary lymph nodes in the spread of cancer? Explain.

The spread of cancer is called metastasis. If a cancerous cell breaks away from its tumour and gets into the lymphatic system, it can potentially reach any part of the body.

The breast drains to the axillary nodes, in the armpit, so they're checked for cancer cells. The first node in line, closest to the breast, is the sentinel node. Cancer cells there show the cancer has spread. No cancer cells there gives some confidence that it's still limited to the breast.

[pause 1]

Q: Two marks. Why did Mary develop swelling in her right arm after the mastectomy? Think about fluid dynamics, and what keeps fluid in the tissue spaces.

When the lymph nodes are removed, the fluid that normally drains from the arm through those nodes is blocked. Normally, tissue fluid drains through the lymphatic vessels, and back into the venous system at the subclavian vein. Now it has nowhere to go. The lymphatic vessels fill until they can't drain the tissue, and fluid stays in the interstitial spaces. That swelling is lymphoedema.

[pause 1]

Q: One mark. What term is used to describe difficulty breathing?

Dyspnoea. Dys means bad, and pnoea means breathing. Compare eupnoea, which is normal breathing. Tachypnoea, fast. Apnoea, none at all. And hyperventilation, breathing hard and fast, which changes the blood's carbon dioxide.

Airway resistance is high, so it's harder to breathe. It takes a greater pressure difference to move the air, and that's the dyspnoea. And the accessory respiratory muscles are recruited: extra muscles for breathing. Her phrase for it is an increase in the work of breathing.

Q: One mark. How does an inhaler help when Ane is having an asthma attack?

A reliever inhaler, like Salbutamol, is a bronchodilator. It forces the smooth muscle of the airways to relax, which lets the airway widen, and that relieves the symptoms so Ane can breathe. Don't mix it up with a preventer, which is a corticosteroid: an anti-inflammatory.

Q: Three marks. How does the body usually keep dust and small particles out of the lungs? There are several mechanisms.

The hook: stick and sweep, filter, cough.

Mucus and cilia: the muco-ciliary escalator. Nasal hairs: a filter that traps particles, which end up stuck in mucus. And the carina, at the bottom of the trachea, which sets off the cough reflex. The epiglottis keeps large objects out of the trachea, but it's no help against small particles.

Q: Two marks. The muco-ciliary escalator is one of the mechanisms. How does it work?

Cilia, tiny hair-like structures lining the respiratory epithelium, beat to sweep mucus up and out of the airways. And mucus is sticky: dust and particles get stuck in it and trapped, and the cilia carry them up and out with it.

Her formative test gives it a tag line: the mechanism formed by the pseudostratified columnar epithelium lining the airways is the muco-ciliary escalator.

Q: Two marks. Smoking damages this escalator. How would this affect a smoker?

The cilia are damaged, so mucus can't be moved up and out of the lungs. And a smoker's cough develops, to replace the lost function of the cilia.

[pause 1]

Q: Four marks. Ane's blood pressure was high because of the fight or flight response. What process helps bring it back to normal after the attack? Think the full process.

This is the baroreceptor reflex. Her recorded session talks it through, and she says think the full process, so give every link.

The hook: stretch, medulla, brake on, accelerator off, vagus slows the heart, vessels widen.

One. The carotid and aortic baroreceptors, which are stretch receptors in the blood vessel walls, pick up that the blood pressure is high. The walls are very stretched.

Two. They send the message to the medulla oblongata, to its cardio-acceleratory, cardio-inhibitory and vasomotor centres.

Three. The cardio-inhibitory centre is stimulated, and the cardio-acceleratory centre is inhibited. As she puts it, you can't brake and accelerate at the same time.

Four. The message goes down the vagus nerve. The rate and the force of the heartbeat drop, so the cardiac output drops.

Five. The vasomotor centre brings about vasodilation: more space for the blood.

And six: together, that brings the blood pressure back down to normal.

# Case 4, part 1: Manaia's breathing

Q: Exam case 4. Panic attacks.

Q: Manaia lives with an anxiety disorder, which sometimes shows up as panic attacks that affect her breathing. She describes a panic attack as a time when she breathes really, really hard and fast, and can't control her breathing at all. There's a link between breathing and the pH of the blood, because the respiratory system is one of the body's buffer systems. During a prolonged panic attack, Manaia might be at risk of respiratory alkalosis, and you'll need to remember why.

Case 4 is the other big one, so it's three tracks as well. First, the mechanics of breathing.

Q: Four marks. Bullet point the steps involved in Manaia's inhalation.

The hook: contract, bigger, lower pressure, air in.

The diaphragm contracts, so its dome lowers. The external intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs up and out. That enlarges the thoracic cavity, and reduces the pressure in the intrapleural cavity. That's Boyle's law. The lungs follow, so the intrapulmonary volume increases, and the intrapulmonary pressure falls. Air flows in from the atmosphere, from high pressure to low.

[pause 1]

Q: Four marks. Bullet point the steps involved in Manaia's exhalation.

The hook: relax, recoil, higher pressure, air out.

The diaphragm relaxes, so its dome rises. The external intercostals relax, so the ribs move down and in. The elastic lungs recoil passively, reducing the intrapulmonary volume. Intrapulmonary pressure rises: Boyle's law again. And air flows out of the lungs, from the higher intrapulmonary pressure to the lower atmospheric pressure.

[pause 1]

Q: Two marks. Manaia feels tired after a panic attack, because forced breathing takes more muscular effort. Which muscles are used in forced breathing? Be specific.

Breathing in. At rest, the diaphragm and the external intercostals contract. When it's forced, the neck muscles join in: the sternocleidomastoid, and the scalenes.

Breathing out. At rest it's passive: the diaphragm and external intercostals just relax. When it's forced, the abdominal muscles and the internal intercostals contract.

So the hook: breathing in, the neck lifts. Breathing out, the abs and the internal intercostals squeeze.

[pause 1]

Q: Three marks. What are some of the factors that influence pulmonary ventilation, making it easier or harder, and how do they do this?

The hook: blocked airways, stiff lungs, and a lost pleural seal.

Blocked airways. Obstruction by fluid, mucus or swelling, as in COPD and asthma, narrows the airways. That raises the resistance to airflow, and the muscular effort needed to breathe in and out.

Stiff lungs. Lung compliance is the ease with which the lungs expand, and scar tissue reduces it. So do problems with the thoracic cage, like an inflexible rib cage or a diaphragm problem. And so do problems in the alveoli: a lack of surfactant, so the alveoli don't expand, or fluid building up in them.

The pleural seal. The negative pressure in the pleural space couples the lungs to the chest wall. Air in the pleural cavity, a pneumothorax, breaks that seal, and the lung collapses. That's atelectasis.

Q: Once more. Manaia's breathing.

[pause 2]

{recap} Inhalation: the diaphragm and external intercostals contract, the thoracic cavity enlarges, the intrapleural and then intrapulmonary pressures fall, by Boyle's law, and air flows in from high pressure to low. Exhalation: they relax, the elastic lungs recoil passively, intrapulmonary pressure rises, and air flows out. Forced breathing in adds the sternocleidomastoid and scalenes; forced breathing out uses the abdominal muscles and internal intercostals. Ventilation gets harder with blocked airways, with stiff lungs from scar tissue or no surfactant, and with a lost pleural seal: a pneumothorax collapses the lung.

# Case 4, part 2: breathing and blood pH

Q: Exam case 4, continued. Breathing and blood pH.

One idea runs through this whole track, so get it first. Carbon dioxide and hydrogen ions move together. Whatever happens to one happens to the other. And more hydrogen ions means a lower pH.

Q: One mark. What is normal blood pH?

Seven point three five to seven point four five, averaging seven point four. Below seven point three five is acidotic. Above seven point four five is alkalotic.

Q: One mark. Write the chemical equation, in words or in formulas, for the bicarbonate buffer system in the blood.

Carbon dioxide plus water, in balance with carbonic acid, in balance with hydrogen ions plus bicarbonate ions. In formulas: C O 2 plus H 2 O, to H 2 C O 3, to H plus, plus H C O 3 minus. And the arrows go both ways.

[pause 1]

Q: Three marks. How do changes in the respiratory rate and depth, like in Manaia's panic attack, affect the blood pH?

The hook: blow off carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions fall, pH rises. Alkalosis.

Changing the rate and depth of breathing changes the carbon dioxide level in the alveoli, and so in the blood. Blood carbon dioxide directly drives the bicarbonate buffer equation, so the same change happens to the hydrogen ion level. And a change in hydrogen ions is a change in pH.

So in Manaia's hyperventilation, she blows off carbon dioxide. The buffer uses up hydrogen ions and bicarbonate to replace it. Hydrogen ions fall, so the pH rises. That's respiratory alkalosis. Hypoventilation, after a head injury for example, does the opposite: carbon dioxide up, hydrogen ions up, pH down. Respiratory acidosis.

[pause 1]

Q: Two marks. How does the respiratory system respond when something causes the blood pH to change? Think back to ketoacidosis in B N 1.

Now it's the other way round: the pH problem comes first, and breathing fixes it.

Metabolic acidosis, with hydrogen ions up, like in ketoacidosis. The body responds with hyperventilation. Hydrogen ions are converted into carbon dioxide and water, and the extra carbon dioxide is breathed off. The breathing rate increases.

Metabolic alkalosis, with hydrogen ions down. The body responds with hypoventilation. Carbon dioxide is kept in the body, and turned into hydrogen ions and bicarbonate, to replace the ones that were lost. The breathing rate decreases.

The same buffer works both ways. Too acid, breathe faster. Too alkaline, breathe slower.

Q: Once more. Breathing and blood pH.

[pause 2]

{recap} Normal blood pH is seven point three five to seven point four five. The buffer: carbon dioxide plus water, carbonic acid, hydrogen ions plus bicarbonate, both ways. Breathing sets the blood's carbon dioxide, and hydrogen ions follow it, so pH follows too. Manaia's hyperventilation blows off carbon dioxide, hydrogen ions fall, and pH rises: respiratory alkalosis. Hypoventilation is the reverse: respiratory acidosis. In metabolic acidosis the body hyperventilates, to breathe off the extra carbon dioxide; in metabolic alkalosis it hypoventilates, to keep it.

Hyperventilation, which is Manaia. Carbon dioxide in the blood decreases. The buffer tries to replace the lost carbon dioxide by using up hydrogen ions and bicarbonate, so hydrogen ions decrease. Blood pH increases: alkalosis.

Hypoventilation, as after head trauma. Carbon dioxide in the blood increases. The buffer gets rid of the extra carbon dioxide by combining it with water and converting it into hydrogen ions and bicarbonate, so hydrogen ions increase. Blood pH decreases: acidosis.
