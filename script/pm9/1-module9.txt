Q: Define a licit drug and an illicit drug, with an example of each. Two marks.

The hook: licit is lawful. Illicit is illegal. And every Class A drug is illicit.

A licit drug is a medication or drug that is lawful and permitted. That covers every schedule of medicine you met in Module 1: prescription medicines, restricted medicines, pharmacy-only medicines and general sale medicines. And it covers some controlled drugs, some from Class B and Class C, because those are prescribed. Licit drugs can still be misused. They just aren't illegal. Her examples: tobacco and nicotine, including vapes. Alcohol. Anti-anxiety medicines like benzodiazepines. Analgesics like the opioids. Cough and cold medicines like pseudoephedrine. And inhalants and solvents.

An illicit drug is unlawful, or otherwise not permitted. That's every Class A controlled drug, some Class B, and a few Class C. Methamphetamine is the example she uses, and it's Class A.

[pause 1]

Remember the classes from the legal lecture. The Misuse of Drugs Act 1975 sorts controlled drugs into Class A, B and C. The higher the class, the higher the risk of misuse and addiction. Class A, very high risk, never prescribed. Class B, like morphine, prescribed, but locked in the double-locked safe. Class C, lower risk, like codeine.

Where the marks go missing. One: controlled does not mean illicit. Her own formative asks this. Controlled drugs are substances under special legal controls to reduce misuse and abuse. Morphine is controlled, and it's licit when it's prescribed. Two: one more word she uses. All medications are drugs, but not all drugs are medications. In Aotearoa, when the use is a worry, people often say substance.

Drug abuse. Self-administration of a drug or substance in chronically excessive amounts, in a way that differs from approved medical use or the usual social patterns of the culture, and that results in physical or psychological harm. Three parts: chronically excessive, outside the norm, and harm. Example: drinking heavily every day, until it damages the person's health, work or relationships.

The difference. Misuse is about the purpose being outside the guidelines. Abuse adds the amount, chronically excessive, and the harm. And her notes add one thing: what counts as abuse is defined by what a society accepts, its own laws, history, religion and ethos.

# Tolerance, withdrawal, and substance use disorder
@ crit: LO1
@ say: Tolerance and withdrawal
@ ask: Define drug tolerance and give an example. (2 marks) || Repeated doses cause decreasing effects, or the dose must be increased for the same effect (receptor downregulation) | Example: morphine, tolerance develops rapidly; marijuana causes little; not every drug causes it
@ ask: What is withdrawal? Give an example of its symptoms. (2 marks) || Symptoms when a physically dependent person stops or cuts down; mild to severe, days to weeks | Depends on the substance, how long, age and health, weaned or cold turkey | Example: insomnia, fatigue, irritability, cravings; severe: paranoia, confusion, tremors
@ ask: What is a substance use disorder, and how is it diagnosed? (2 marks) || A mental health condition diagnosed with the DSM-5 | By the number of its eleven criteria met: more criteria, more severe (mild, moderate, severe) | Criteria include cravings, tolerance, withdrawal

Q: Define drug tolerance, with an example. Then: what is withdrawal, and what is a substance use disorder?

The hook: tolerance, same dose, less effect. Withdrawal, stop it and the body protests. Substance use disorder, the manual counts the criteria.

Tolerance. A physical state in which repeated doses of a drug cause decreasing effects, or the dose must be increased to keep the same effect. Her slide reminds you of the mechanism from pharmacodynamics: receptor downregulation. And tolerance doesn't happen with every drug. Her example: morphine users develop tolerance rapidly, but marijuana causes little tolerance. So the example to write: a patient on regular morphine needs higher doses for the same pain relief.

[pause 1]

Withdrawal. This slide has Pusheen on it, so learn the idea, not the list. Withdrawal is the set of symptoms when a person who is physically dependent stops or cuts down. It ranges from mild to severe, and lasts days or weeks, depending on the substance, how long it was used, the person's age, other conditions and mental state, and whether it was weaned slowly or stopped cold turkey. A few examples are enough: insomnia, fatigue, irritability and cravings. Severe withdrawal can bring paranoia, confusion, tremors and disorientation.

Substance use disorder. Pusheen again on the details. There's a mental health manual called the DSM 5, the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, and clinicians use it to diagnose substance use disorder. The diagnosis, and how severe it is, depends on how many of its criteria a person meets. More criteria, more severe. Two of the criteria are words you already know: needing more for the same effect, which is tolerance, and withdrawal symptoms. Others are cravings, and not managing to cut down.

Q: Once more. Tolerance, withdrawal, and substance use disorder.

[pause 2]

{recap} Tolerance: repeated doses give decreasing effects, so the dose has to go up for the same effect, through receptor downregulation. Morphine, rapidly; marijuana, hardly at all. Withdrawal: the symptoms when a dependent person stops, like insomnia, irritability and cravings, worse when stopped cold turkey than weaned. Substance use disorder is diagnosed with the DSM 5, by how many criteria are met: more criteria, more severe.

# Stimulants and depressants
@ crit: LO2
@ say: Stimulant or depressant
@ ask: Is it a stimulant, a depressant, or both? (concept) || Stimulants, uppers, speed up the body and brain: MDMA, methamphetamine | Depressants, downers, slow them down: alcohol, GHB, sedatives such as lorazepam and zopiclone, nitrous oxide, inhalants | Both: nicotine, marijuana, psychedelics, synthetic cannabinoids

Q: Stimulant, or depressant? What's the difference?

Pusheen is on these slides, so this is the concept. Stimulants and depressants are the two main groups of mind-altering drugs, and they act in opposite ways on the central nervous system. Stimulants, uppers, speed up the body and the brain. Depressants, downers, slow them down, so they calm, sedate, and make people drowsy.

On her chart. Stimulants: MDMA, and methamphetamine. Depressants: alcohol, GHB, sedatives such as lorazepam and zopiclone, nitrous oxide, and inhalants like glue, gas and petrol. Both stimulant and depressant: nicotine, marijuana, psychedelics, and synthetic cannabinoids.

Why a nurse cares. Two depressants together slow breathing more than either alone. That's why mixing alcohol with an opioid, or with benzodiazepines, is so dangerous. You'll hear that again in the interactions track.

She also showed slang on Pusheen slides, so it's not for marks, but it's useful on the ward. P is methamphetamine. Four twenty is marijuana. Lean is codeine in a fizzy drink. Pingers are ecstasy. Fishies are GHB. Nangs are nitrous oxide. And acid is LSD.

Q: Once more. Stimulants and depressants.

[pause 2]

{recap} Stimulants speed up the body and brain: MDMA, methamphetamine. Depressants slow them down: alcohol, GHB, sedatives, nitrous oxide, inhalants. Some are both: nicotine, marijuana, psychedelics, synthetic cannabinoids. Two depressants together, like alcohol and an opioid, are the dangerous mix.

# Antidotes: naloxone and N-acetylcysteine
@ crit: LO5
@ say: Antidotes
@ ask: Define an antidote and give an example. (2 marks) || A substance that counteracts a poison: binding it (activated charcoal), slowing or speeding an enzyme, or blocking its receptors (an antagonist) | Naloxone (Narcan): an opioid antagonist, reverses an opioid overdose | N-acetylcysteine (NAC): reduces the toxic metabolites made from paracetamol

Q: Define an antidote, and give an example. Two marks.

The hook: naloxone blocks the opioid. N A C protects the liver from paracetamol.

An antidote is a substance that counteracts a poison. Her slide gives four ways an antidote can work. One, binders, which reduce the level of free toxin. Activated charcoal is the example. Two, enzyme inhibition: slowing an enzyme's action. Three, enzyme activation: speeding it up. And four, blocking receptors, which is an antagonist. That word should ring a bell from pharmacodynamics: an agonist binds a receptor and causes a response, and an antagonist binds and blocks it.

[pause 1]

Her two examples. Naloxone, brand name Narcan, works as an opioid antagonist. It blocks the opioid receptors, so it reverses an opioid overdose. And N-acetylcysteine, N A C, reduces the production of the toxic metabolites made from paracetamol, so it's the antidote for paracetamol overdose.

Where the marks go missing. Naloxone is an antagonist, not an agonist. It blocks the receptor, it doesn't stimulate it. And N A C doesn't remove paracetamol from the body. It reduces the toxic metabolites the liver makes from it.

Q: Once more. Antidotes.

[pause 2]

{recap} An antidote counteracts a poison: by binding it, like activated charcoal; by slowing or speeding an enzyme; or by blocking its receptors, as an antagonist. Naloxone, Narcan, is an opioid antagonist that reverses an opioid overdose. N-acetylcysteine, N A C, reduces the toxic metabolites made from paracetamol.
